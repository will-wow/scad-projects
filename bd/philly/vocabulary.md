# Vocabulary

The words this model uses for the parts of a boat, and why.

*Philadelphia* was built in 1776, so where an 18th-century shipwright had a word
for a thing, that is the word the code uses. This is not decoration. A knee is
not a bracket, a floor is not a surface, and a truck is not a carriage — and when
the code says `bracket` or `floor` or `truck` loosely, it stops being able to say
the precise thing later. The gun got this treatment first and it worked: the
cannon reads clearly because `cascabel`, `trunnion` and `quoin` each mean exactly
one thing.

Read this before naming anything new. If a word is not here and you are unsure,
prefer plain English over a nautical-sounding guess — the false friends below are
all words that *sound* right and are not.

## Confidence

| Code | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **[G]** | Solid 18th-century British shipwright or seaman's usage, attested in Falconer (1769/80), Murray (1754) or Steel (1805) |
| **[G·boat]** | Georgian *and* specifically a small-craft word — first choice for this vessel |
| **[G·ship]** | Georgian, but belongs to a ship of the line. A 53-foot lake gunboat had no such thing |
| **[V]** | Victorian — first firm attestation around Smyth (1867). Usable, not Georgian |
| **[A]** | American or colonial |
| **[M]** | Modern, engineering or yachting. Avoid |

## The vessel

53 ft 2 in by 15 ft 2 in, about 2 ft draught, 29 tons burthen. Oak,
flat-bottomed, open. One 36-ft pole mast carrying a square course and a square
topsail. Twelve sweeps of 20½ ft. A 12-pounder forward on a sliding carriage, two
9-pounders in broadside. A canvas awning abaft the mast. Built without draughts,
to written dimensions from Benedict Arnold.

Her own papers call this type a **gondola** or **gundelo** [G·A]. *Gundalow* is
the New England form that historians generalised later. *Gunboat* [G] is fine.
*Bateau* [G·A] is a different, smaller type.

## Settled terms

These are already in the code and correct. Match them.

**Hull form and the lines** — `sheer` (the longitudinal curve of the deck or
sides, seen in profile), `chine` (where the flat bottom meets the side),
`station` (a frame's fore-and-aft position), `breadth` (the width; *beam* only in
phrases like "on the beam"), `depth`, `forefoot` (the curve where bottom meets
stem), `stem`, `transom`, `rake`, `bow`, `rail`, `amidships`, `inboard` /
`outboard`, `port` / `starboard`, `proud` (standing above a surface).

**Structure and joinery** — `planking` (the hull's skin; its thickness, not
"wall"), `strake` (one continuous line of planking), `keelson` (the timber over
the keel, binding the floors down to it), `knee` (a crooked timber connecting a
beam to the side; its inside corner is the **throat**, its outside the **back**),
`beam` (a transverse timber under a deck), `platform` (a *partial* deck — which is
what all three of this boat's decks are), `ceiling` (the inside planking over the
bottom; *not* a floor), `thwart` (a transverse seat), `bench`, `well` (an open
stretch), `bulwark`, `bilge`, `forecastle`, `quarterdeck`, `chock` (a block or
wedge securing a gun or a spar).

**A timber's two dimensions** — **sided** (or `siding`) is the dimension athwart
the timber; **moulded** is the depth in the plane of the mould. These are the
period names for exactly the two parameters a rectangular section needs, and the
code uses them in preference to `thickness` and `height`. Both [G], Steel.

**Rig** — `mast`, `mast thwart` (on a boat, the thwart bored to take the mast,
doing the partners' work — one member, one name), `step` (the socket the heel
stands in), `heel`, `truck` (the cap at the masthead), `yard`, `course` (the
lower square sail), `topsail`, `head` / `foot` / `drop` of a sail, `cringle` (a
rope eye worked into a sail's edge), `bolt rope` (the rope round a sail's edge),
`canvas`, `spar`.

**Awning** — `awning` [G], `stanchion` (Falconer's own word, in the AWNING entry,
for the light posts that carry it), `crown`, `canvas`.

**Guns** — `carriage`, `slide`, `bed`, `bracket` / `cheek` (a carriage's sides),
`transom` (a carriage's cross pieces — note the false friend), `truck` (a
carriage *wheel*), `axletree`, `trunnion`, `quoin` (the elevation wedge) on its
`stool bed`, `capsquare`, `breeching` (the rope limiting recoil), `cascabel`,
`base ring`, `swell`, `muzzle`, `bore`, `calibre`, `bow chaser`, `chase port`
(what a bow gun fires through), `broadside`, `recoil`, `to train` a gun.

## Reaching for a word

What a modeller would say, and what to write instead.

### Hull and framing

| Modern | Period |
|---|---|
| wall, shell, skin thickness | **planking** |
| rib | **timber**, **frame**, **bend** — never *rib* |
| lower rib segment | **floor** / **floor timber** (a timber, not a surface) |
| upper rib segment | **futtock**; the topmost is the **top timber** |
| cross brace, gusset, bracket | **knee** — vertical arm: **hanging knee**; horizontal: **lodging knee**; diagonal: **dagger knee** |
| bow reinforcement | **breast hook** |
| frame spacing, pitch | **room and space** |
| lap joint, splice | **scarph** |
| filler block, shim | **chock** |
| part dimensions | **scantling** |
| section thickness (two axes) | **siding** and **moulded** |
| spine, backbone | **keel**; the inner one, **keelson** |
| plank row | **strake** |
| bottom plank next the keel | **garboard** |
| groove in the keel for plank ends | **rabbet** (the shipwright's spelling; *rebate* is the joiner's) |
| gap between planks | **seam**; an end joint is a **butt** |
| sealant | **oakum**, driven as **caulking**, then **paid** with pitch |
| dowel, wooden peg | **treenail** (spoken **trunnel**) |
| inner lining, floorboards | **ceiling**, **foot-waling** |
| flush-planked | **carvel-built** |

### Shape and lines

| Modern | Period |
|---|---|
| profile view, side elevation | **sheer draught** |
| plan view | **half-breadth plan** |
| section view | **body plan** |
| widest section, master section | **dead flat** (marked ⊗), **the midship bend** |
| centreline | **the middle line** |
| spline, fitted curve | **a sweep** — an arc struck with compass or batten |
| curve of the deck, transverse | **the round-up of the beam** |
| curve of the deck, lengthwise | **the sheer** |
| V of the bottom | **dead rising**, **the rising line of the floor** |
| inward lean of the sides | **tumbling home** |
| outward lean of the sides | **flaring** |
| overall width | **the extreme breadth** |
| width to the frames, no plank | **the moulded breadth** |
| template, sketch profile | **mould** |
| full-scale layout | **laying off**, on the **mould loft** floor |
| face angle, draft angle | **bevelling** — obtuse is **out-square**, acute **under-square** |
| fairing batten | **riband**; the stiff ones at the ends, **harpins** |

| Modern | Period |
|---|---|
| side wall | **bulwark** (ship) / **washboard** (boat) |
| top edge rail | **gunwale** (spoken **gunnel**) |
| cross seat | **thwart** |
| seat support ledger | **rising** |
| floor panel, sole | **platform**, **flooring**, **foot-waling** |
| partial deck | **platform** |
| raised edge round an opening | **coaming** |
| grille, lattice cover | **grating** |

| vertical post | **stanchion** |

| oar | **sweep** on this vessel; the act is **to pull** |

### Rig

| Modern | Period |
|---|---|
| mast socket, base hole | **the step** |
| deck collar round a mast | **the partners**; on a boat, the **mast thwart** |
| mast top cap | **the truck** |
| crossbar carrying a sail | **yard** |
| yard-to-mast collar | **parrel** |
| hoisting line | **halliard** (period spelling); a topsail's upper part is the **tye** |
| yard tip support line | **lift** |
| yard rotation line | **brace** |
| sail corner lines | **sheet** aft, **tack** forward |
| sail corner lashing | **earing**, through a **cringle** |
| rigging tensioner | **deadeye** and **laniard**; for a stay, a **heart** |
| side stay | **shroud** |
| fore-and-aft stay | **stay**, **forestay** |
| sail's vertical dimension | **depth** [G] — *drop* is period too, but Steel defines it as "sometimes used … instead of depth" |
| reinforcing at a sail's lower corner | **clue-piece** |
| reinforcing at a sail's head corner | **earing-piece** |
| hem round a sail's edge | **tabling** — *not* the corner |
| broad reinforcing band across a sail | **lining** — *leech-lining*, *top-lining*, *buntline-cloth* |

A single corner of a square sail is four distinct things: the **earing-piece** or
**clue-piece** (the patch), the **cringle** (the eye worked into it), the **bolt
rope** (the edging it is seized to), and the **earing** (the lashing that takes
it to the yard). The model prints one identical patch at all four corners, which
no period word covers — Steel names them by which corner they sit at — so the
code says `patch` and makes no claim.

### Awning

Falconer's AWNING entry (1769) is the authority, and it names two of these
outright:

> "a canopy of canvass extending over the decks of a ship in hot weather … The
> awning is supported by a range of light posts, called **stanchions**, which are
> erected along the ship's side on the right and left; it is also suspended in the
> middle by a complication of small cords, called a **crowfoot**."

| Modern | Period |
|---|---|
| canopy, sunshade, cover | **awning**; over a boat's stern sheets, a **tilt** [V] |
| tent pole, vertical support | **stanchion** [G] |
| forked support, Y-bracket | **crotch** (**crutch**) |
| ridge beam | **the ridge**; the hoops bearing up a tilt are **bails** [V] |
| the canvas's top surface | *no period word* — describe it as a height, or the height of the stanchions |
| centre suspension lines | **crowfoot** [G] — *ridge rope* is [V] |
| guy line, edge line | **side ropes**, **lacings** |
| corner tie-down | **earing**, through a **cringle** or **eyelet hole** |
| small lashing | **stops** of rope-yarn, **seizings** |
| side screen, spray shield | **weather cloth** |
| bundled-stick armour | **fascines** — she probably carried them along her sides |

## False friends

Words that sound right and are not. Several of these were reached for during the
audit and rejected.

| Avoid | Why | Use |
|---|---|---|
| **tumblehome** | one-word noun is modern | **tumbling home** |
| **flare** (noun) | Victorian as a noun | **flaring** — the verb is fine: "the side flares" |
| **deadrise** | modern powerboat | **dead rising**, the **rising line of the floor** |
| **buttock lines** | 19th-c. draughting | **diagonals** and **horizontal lines** |
| **camber** (of a deck) | in the 18th c. usually meant the keel's fore-and-aft arc | **the round-up of the beam** |
| **table of offsets** | 19th-c. convention | **the draught**, **the scantling list** |
| **freeboard** | 19th-c. | "the height of the side above water" |
| **oarlock**, metal **rowlock** | Victorian fitting / American | **row-lock** as a notch, or **thole pins** |
| **cap rail**, **inwale**, **outwale**, **covering board** | boatbuilding and yachting | **gunwale**, **washboard**, **rising**, **plank-sheer** |
| **fairlead** | 19th-c. | **bull's-eye**, **leading block**, **sheave hole** |
| **stringer** | iron shipbuilding | **clamp**, **shelf**, **sleeper**, **thick stuff** |
| **chine log** | 19th-c. American scow work | describe it, or **chine piece** with a caveat |
| **companionway** | later compound, and this boat has no cabin | **scuttle**, **hatchway** |
| **rib** | layman's word | **timber**, **frame**, **futtock** |
| **porthole** | modern | **port** (gun), **light** or **scuttle** (window) |
| **deckhead**, **cabin sole** | yachting | "the underside of the deck"; **the platform** |
| **halyard**, **lanyard**, **rebate** | modern spellings | **halliard**, **laniard**, **rabbet** |
| **spreaders**, **jackstay** (of an awning) | not period at all | **bails**, **crowfoot** |
| **crown** (of an awning) | the nautical senses are the crown of an *anchor* and *crowning* a rope's end; Falconer gives no word for an awning's top | a height, not a part |
| **transom** meaning the flat stern | modern shift — in 1776 *transoms* are the timbers across the sternpost, and a gun carriage's cross pieces | **the stern**, **the fashion pieces** |
| **truck** meaning a carriage | a truck is a carriage *wheel*, or a masthead cap | **carriage** |
| **rig** meaning anything but masts and sails | a rig is a vessel's masts, spars and sails | name the thing |
| **floor** meaning a surface | a floor is a transverse bottom timber | **ceiling**, **foot-waling** |
| **frame** meaning any framework | a frame is a transverse hull timber | name the thing |
| **bearing** meaning any angle | a bearing is a compass direction from the observer | **training** (a gun), or the plan angle |
| **to row** | landsman's verb | **to pull** |

Also genuinely Georgian, but belonging to a ship rather than to a flat-bottomed
open lake gunboat built in eight weeks: **wales**, **scuppers**, **channels**,
**cant timbers**, **deadwood**, **jeers**, **companion**, **false keel**. If one
of these turns up in the code it is probably describing something simpler.

## Where the words stop

The boat gets period words. The machine does not.

`as_part`, `on_the_bed`, `_loft`, `DEBRIS_FRACTION`, `MESH_TOLERANCE`,
`TOLERANCE`, `STEM_LIFT`, `STEM_OVERLAP`, `SOCKET_DEPTH`, the chamfers, and all
of `preview.py`, `export.py` and `watch.py` describe solid modelling and 3D
printing. They are named for what they are. Keeping that line visible is
deliberate: when a name is modern you can tell at a glance that it is a print
concern and not something a shipwright would recognise.

`Scaled` is the same case. A shipwright interrogating his draught for offsets had
a **table of offsets** — but that is a 19th-century convention, and in 1776 he had
a draught and a scantling list. `Scaled` honestly describes arithmetic, so it
stays.

## Sources

- William Falconer, *An Universal Dictionary of the Marine* (1769; 1780 edn.) —
  the best single source. Full text at Project Gutenberg and whalesite.org.
- David Steel, *Elements and Practice of Naval Architecture* (1805), "An
  Explanation of the Terms Used in Ship Building" — transcription at
  hmssurprise.org. Best on timbers, framing and ironwork.
- David Steel, *The Elements and Practice of Rigging and Seamanship* (1794),
  part 4 — transcription at legacy.maritime.org. A *sailmaker's* text, and far
  better than the 1805 book on sails: it is the source for `depth`, the
  corner pieces and the linings above.
- Mungo Murray, *A Treatise on Shipbuilding and Navigation* (1754).
- W. H. Smyth, *The Sailor's Word-Book* (1867) — used mainly to *date* terms:
  what appears here but not in Falconer is Victorian, not Georgian.
- Smithsonian NMAH, the Gunboat *Philadelphia* Preservation Project; Lake
  Champlain Maritime Museum.
- Philip Lundeberg, *The Gunboat Philadelphia and the Defense of Lake Champlain
  in 1776*, and the NHL nomination for *Philadelphia* (NRHP 66000852) — the two
  documents that would give her *recorded* member names and scantlings. Neither
  has been mined yet; start there if you need the real counts.
