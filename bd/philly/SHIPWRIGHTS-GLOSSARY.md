# A shipwright's glossary, c. 1769–1805

A working reference for naming parts in models of 18th-century vessels. Broader
than any one boat: it carries ship-scale terms the *Philadelphia* never had, the
small-craft words she did, and the dates at which words stop being Georgian.

For what *this* model calls things, see [vocabulary.md](vocabulary.md). That file
is the decided house style; this one is the raw material behind it, kept so the
next model does not have to re-derive it.

## How to use this

Each term carries a confidence code. **Check it before you use the word** — about
a fifth of the nautical vocabulary that feels period is Victorian, and a modern
word honestly used is better than a period word used wrongly.

| Code | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **[G]** | Solid 18th-c. British shipwright or seaman's usage, attested in Falconer (1769/80), Murray (1754), Steel (1794 or 1805) |
| **[G·boat]** | Georgian *and* specifically a small-craft word — first choice for boats, bateaux, gundalows, river craft |
| **[G·ship]** | Georgian, but belongs to a ship of the line or large merchantman. A small open vessel had no such thing |
| **[V]** | Victorian — first firm attestation around Smyth (1867). Usable, not Georgian |
| **[A]** | American or colonial |
| **[M]** | Modern, engineering or yachting. Avoid |
| **[?]** | Plausible, unverified in a period source |

Two standing rules, learned the hard way:

1. **A period word that is wrong about the object is worse than a modern word
   that is right.** A straight crossbar is not a *bail*, because bails are hoops.
2. **The vessel gets period words; the workshop does not.** Tolerances, mesh
   settings, boolean operations, print orientation and slicer perimeters are not
   things a shipwright had opinions about. Leaving them modern is what lets a
   reader tell at a glance which concern a name belongs to.

## 1. Backbone and framing

| Term | Definition | Confidence |
|---|---|---|
| **Keel** | The lowest and principal timber, running the whole length, on which the frame is raised. | [G] |
| **False keel** | A sacrificial strip bolted under the keel. | [G·ship] |
| **Keelson**, **kelson** | Squared timbers inside the hull over the keel, binding the floor timbers down to it. Steel: for "binding and strengthing the lower part." *Kelson* is the commoner 18th-c. spelling. | [G] |
| **Stem** | The timber at the fore end into which the sides are united. | [G] |
| **Stem head** | The top of the stem, standing above the sheer. What a cable or forestay belays to on a small craft. | [G] |
| **Sternpost** | The upright timber aft; carries the rudder on gudgeons and pintles. | [G] |
| **Apron** | Falconer: curved timber fixed behind the lower part of the stem, above the foremost end of the keel. An inner stem backing the scarph. | [G] |
| **Stemson** | The inner piece above the apron, uniting apron to keelson. | [G] |
| **Deadwood** | Steel: solid pieces scarfed together lengthwise on the keel, forward and aft. Minimal or absent on a flat-bottomed scow hull. | [G] |
| **Frame**, **bend** | One complete transverse assembly of timbers. *Bend* is the shipwright's everyday word — "the midship bend". | [G] |
| **Timber** | The generic word for a single frame member. Shipwrights say *timber*, never *rib*. | [G] |
| **Floor**, **floor timber** | The lowest timber of a frame, crossing the keel. Steel defines the *floor* as the part of the bottom nearer horizontal than perpendicular. **A floor is a timber, not a surface.** | [G] |
| **Futtock** | Steel: the separate pieces composing a frame, named by position — first futtock, second futtock, upward. | [G] |
| **Top timber** | The uppermost piece of a frame, reaching the sheer. | [G] |
| **Crotches**, **crutches**, **half-floors** | Falconer: crooked timbers on the keel fore and aft, where the body narrows. | [G] |
| **Cant timbers** | Frames standing canted, not square, to the keel near bow and stern. | [G·ship] |
| **Square body**, **cant body** | The length whose frames stand square to the keel, versus the canted ends. | [G] |
| **Room and space** | The period measure of frame spacing: *room* is the width of a timber, *space* the gap. Use this, not "frame pitch". | [G] |
| **Scarph** (not *scarf*) | The long tapered lap joint between two timbers. | [G] |
| **Chock** | A filling piece let in to make good a gap between timbers or in a scarph. Also, generally, a block or wedge securing a gun, a spar or a boat. | [G] |
| **Compass timber** | Naturally curved oak, grown to the shape wanted. The period reason knees look as they do. | [G] |
| **Knee** | Falconer: a crooked piece of timber with two arms, connecting the beams to the sides or timbers. Its inside corner is the **throat**, its outside the **back**. | [G] |
| **Hanging knee** | Steel: a knee against the side whose arm hangs vertically, under the beam end. | [G] |
| **Lodging knee** | Fixed horizontally, with the hang of the deck, between a beam's side and the ship's side. | [G] |
| **Standing knee** | A knee with its arm standing upward, above the beam. | [G] |
| **Dagger knee** | Steel: knees set obliquely in place of hanging knees, chiefly on merchant lower-deck beams. | [G] |
| **Breast hook** | A horizontal knee across the inside of the stem, binding the bows together. A real boat fitting. | [G·boat] |
| **Beam** | A heavy transverse timber supporting a deck and holding the sides apart. | [G] |
| **Carling**, **carline** | Fore-and-aft pieces framed between the beams. | [G] |
| **Ledge** | Steel: scantling let into the carlings athwartships — the light fillers between beams. Not a shelf. | [G] |
| **Head ledge** | The athwartship piece at the end of a hatchway or framed opening. | [G] |
| **Clamp** | The thick inside strake under the beam ends, on which they rest. | [G·ship] |
| **Shelf** | The inboard timber carrying beam ends in lighter construction. | [G] |
| **Sleeper** | A timber laid lengthwise low in the hold; the general word for a lying bearer. | [G] |
| **Sided**, **siding** | The dimension athwart a timber. | [G] |
| **Moulded** | The dimension in the plane the mould is laid in — the depth seen on the body plan. | [G] |
| **Scantling** | The sectional dimensions of a timber or plank. | [G] |

**Sided and moulded are the two names a rectangular section needs.** They are
the period answer to `thickness` and `height`, and they remove the ambiguity
both of those words carry. Use them.

## 2. Planking and skin

| Term | Definition | Confidence |
|---|---|---|
| **Strake**, **streak** | One continuous line of planking stem to stern. Falconer spells it *streak*. | [G] |
| **Garboard strake** | Steel: the strake of the bottom wrought next the keel, rabbeting into it. | [G] |
| **Rabbet** | The groove cut in keel, stem and sternpost to receive the plank ends. *Rabbet* is the shipwright's spelling; *rebate* is the joiner's. | [G] |
| **Bearding**, **bearding line** | The line on keel or post where the rabbet's inner edge falls. | [G] |
| **Wales**, **main wales** | Extra-thick strakes worked along a ship's side about the waterline, to girdle her. | [G·ship] |
| **Rubbing strake** | A sacrificial strip along a boat's side. | [?] |
| **Sheer strake** | The topmost strake of the side, following the sheer. | [G] |
| **Thick stuff** | Heavy plank, over 4 in and under 12, worked inside over the floor heads and other strong places. | [G] |
| **Ceiling**, **foot-waling** | Falconer: the inside planking or lining; *foot-waling* specifically all the inside planking below the lower deck. **This, not "floor", is the surface you stand on.** | [G] |
| **Spirketting** | The inside strakes between a deck's waterway and the gunports' sills. | [G·ship] |
| **Seam** | The joint between the edges of two adjacent planks. | [G] |
| **Butt** | The end joint where two planks meet in the same strake. | [G] |
| **Caulking**, **calking** | Driving oakum into the seams to keep the water out. | [G] |
| **Oakum** | Old rope picked apart, driven into seams. | [G] |
| **Paying** | Running hot pitch over a caulked seam to finish it. | [G] |
| **Treenail**, **trenail**, **trunnel** | A turned wooden pin driven through plank into timber and wedged. *Trunnel* is the spoken and American form. | [G] |
| **Spike** | A long heavy wrought nail. The ordinary fastening for fast-built American work in 1776. | [G] |
| **Dump** | A short bolt or heavy spike. | [G] |
| **Rose nail**, **clench nail** | Wrought nails; a clench nail is riveted over inside. | [G·boat] |
| **Carvel-built** | Planked flush, edge to edge, over frames, and caulked. | [G] |
| **Clincher-work**, **clinker-built** | Planked with overlapping edges, riveted. Falconer has *clincher-work*. | [G·boat] |
| **Graving piece**, **tingle** | A let-in patch over local damage. | [G] |
| **Sheathing** | Boards or copper over the bottom against worm. Irrelevant in fresh water. | [G·ship] |
| **Chine** | The angle where a flat bottom meets the side. Good old English for the angle; but **chine log**, as a named member, is 19th-c. American scow building. For 1776 describe it, or say **chine piece** with a caveat. | angle [?]; *chine log* [A, later] |

### CAD word → period word

| Modeller's word | Period term |
|---|---|
| hull shell, skin, wall thickness | **the planking** |
| plank row, plank band | **strake** (**streak**) |
| bottom plank next the keel | **garboard** |
| top plank row | **sheer strake** |
| groove in the keel for plank ends | **rabbet** |
| gap between planks | **seam**; end joint, **butt** |
| sealant, gasket | **oakum**, driven as **caulking**, then **paid** with pitch |
| dowel, wooden peg | **treenail** (**trunnel**) |
| inner lining, floorboards | **ceiling**, **foot-waling** |
| heavy internal plank | **thick stuff** |
| flush-planked | **carvel-built** |
| lapped planking | **clincher-work** |

## 3. Shape, lines and lofting

| Term | Definition | Confidence |
|---|---|---|
| **Sheer** | Falconer: the longitudinal curve of a ship's deck or sides, seen in profile. | [G] |
| **Sheer draught**, **plane of elevation** | The side view — the profile drawing. | [G] |
| **Half-breadth plan**, **floor plane** | The horizontal projection, on which the breadths at each timber are set off. | [G] |
| **Body plan**, **plane of projection** | The transverse sections. *Plane of projection* is Falconer's phrase; *body plan* is the shipwright's and is period. | [G] |
| **Dead flat**, marked **⊗** | Steel: the timber or frame of greatest breadth and capacity. Where several are equal, the middle one is reckoned dead flat. **The right name for the widest frame.** | [G] |
| **Midship bend** | Steel: the bend called dead flat. Interchangeable. | [G] |
| **Station** | The fore-and-aft position of a frame or section on the draught. Period practice letters them forward of ⊗ (A, B, C…) and figures them aft (1, 2, 3…). | [G] |
| **Water lines**, **level lines**, **horizontal lines** | Lines determining the body's shape horizontally. **Light water line** and **load water line** are both period. | [G] |
| **Diagonal line** | Steel: a line cutting the body plan diagonally from the timbers to the middle line. Used for fairing. | [G] |
| **Buttock lines** | *Buttock* is solidly period for the **shape of the stern** ("a full buttock"). As a named family of longitudinal vertical sections on a drawing it is essentially 19th-c.; Georgian shipwrights faired with **diagonals** and **horizontal lines**. | shape [G]; drawing convention [V] |
| **Rising line of the floor**, **dead rising** | Steel: the line through those parts of the floor where the sweep at the floor timber's head terminates. | [G] |
| **Narrowing line** | The curve governing how the half-breadths diminish toward the ends. | [G] |
| **Sweep** | **The key 18th-c. shape word**: an arc struck with compass or batten, from which the body was built up — **floor sweep**, **lower-breadth sweep**, **breadth sweep**, **back sweep**, **reconciling sweep**. Period sections are arcs and tangents, not splines. | [G] |
| **Round-up**, **rounding** (of a beam) | The transverse convexity given to a deck beam, measured at the middle. **This is "the curve of the deck".** | [G] |
| **Camber** | In the 18th c. most often the **fore-and-aft arc of the keel**. Ambiguous for a deck — prefer *round-up*. | [G] but ambiguous |
| **Rake** | The inclination of stem or sternpost from the perpendicular. | [G] |
| **Tumbling home**, **tumbling in**, **falling home** | Falconer: that part of a ship's side which falls inward above the extreme breadth. The participle is the period form. | [G] |
| **Flaring** | Steel (FLAIRING): the reverse of falling home, when the topside falls outward from a perpendicular. The Georgian form is the participle. | [G] |
| **Breadth, extreme** | The greatest breadth, outside the planking. | [G] |
| **Breadth, moulded** | The breadth to the outside of the frames — inside the plank. **The distinction a CAD model actually needs.** | [G] |
| **Depth in hold** | From the limber strake to the underside of the deck beams. | [G] |
| **Draught of water** | Depth of immersion. Note the period spelling. | [G] |
| **Topside** | The side above the breadth. Steel uses the singular. | [G] |
| **Middle line** | The centreline of the draught. Period shipwrights say *the middle line*. | [G] |
| **Mould** | Steel: pieces of deal or board made to the shape of the lines on the mould loft floor. The period word for a template. | [G] |
| **Bend mould**, **hollow mould**, **square mould** | The templates for a frame's outer curve, the hollow at its heel, and the bevel. | [G] |
| **Mould loft** | The floor on which the lines were drawn full size. | [G] |
| **Laying off**, **laying down** | Steel: delineating the parts of the ship to true size on the mould-loft floor, from the draught. | [G] |
| **Fairing**, **a fair body** | Making the lines sweet and continuous. | [G] |
| **Bevelling**, **bevellings** | The twist given a frame's faces so the plank lies flush. An obtuse bevel is **out-square**, an acute one **under-square** or **in-square**. **Precisely what a lofting routine computes.** | [G] |
| **Riband**, **ribband** | The limber batten nailed round the erected frames to hold and fair them. The period word for a fairing batten. | [G] |
| **Harpin** | Steel: like ribbands but trimmed and bevelled to the body's shape — the stiff ribbands at the ends. | [G] |
| **Scrieve board** | A loft board with the body plan scored into it. Firm in 19th-c. practice; not in Steel 1805. | [V/?] |
| **Table of offsets** | A tabulated set of half-breadths and heights. 19th-c. draughting convention. In 1776 you have a **draught** and a **scantling list**. | [V] |

### CAD word → period word

| Modeller's word | Period term |
|---|---|
| profile view, side elevation | **sheer draught**, **plane of elevation** |
| plan view, top view | **half-breadth plan**, **floor plane** |
| section view, cross-sections | **body plan**, **plane of projection** |
| widest section, master section | **dead flat (⊗)**, **the midship bend** |
| section index, station number | **station** |
| horizontal section cuts | **water lines**, **level lines** |
| vertical longitudinal cuts | **diagonals** (not *buttocks*, in 1776) |
| centreline | **the middle line** |
| spline, fitted curve | **a sweep** |
| curve of the deck, transverse | **the round-up of the beam** |
| curve of the deck, lengthwise | **the sheer** |
| V of the bottom | **dead rising**, **the rising line of the floor** |
| inward lean of the sides | **tumbling home** |
| outward lean of the sides | **flaring** |
| post angle, tilt of stem | **rake** |
| overall width | **the extreme breadth** |
| width to frames, no plank | **the moulded breadth** |
| template, sketch profile | **mould** |
| full-scale layout | **laying off**, on the **mould loft** |
| face angle, draft angle | **bevelling** (**out-square**, **under-square**) |
| fairing batten, loft spline | **riband**; stiff ones at the ends, **harpins** |
| displacement tonnage | **tons burthen** |

## 4. Deck and interior

| Term | Definition | Confidence |
|---|---|---|
| **Deck** | A continuous floor across the vessel. | [G] |
| **Platform** | Falconer's sense: a **partial** deck or flooring, not a full one. The right word for local flooring that crew and guns stand on. | [G] |
| **Thwart** | Falconer: the seat or bench of a boat whereon the rowers sit. **Use this, not "bench", for a rowing seat.** | [G·boat] |
| **Mast thwart** | The thwart bored to take the mast, doing the partners' work. One member, one name. | [G·boat] |
| **Bench** | Period but generic; Falconer uses it of the stern-sheets seats. | [G] |
| **Stern sheets** | Falconer: the part of a boat between the stern and the aftmost rowing seat, generally furnished with benches. | [G·boat] |
| **Rising**, **risings** | A narrow strake fastened inside to support the thwarts. Firmly attested by the 19th c., very likely older; not a Falconer headword. | [V, probably earlier] |
| **Foot-waling** | The inside lining; the boards underfoot. Prefer this to *bottom boards*. | [G] |
| **Bottom boards** | Loose boards in a boat's bottom. Standard boat English, not a Falconer headword. | [V/?] |
| **Grating** | Falconer: open covers for the hatches, of small laths crossing at right angles with square intervals. | [G] |
| **Hatch**, **hatchway** | The opening in a deck, and its cover. | [G] |
| **Scuttle** | A small square hole or hatch with a lid. Better than *hatch* for a small opening in a boat's short deck. | [G] |
| **Coamings**, **combings** | Falconer: raised borders about the edge of the hatches, to keep deck water from going below. | [G] |
| **Waterway** | The channel or thickened strake at the deck's edge where it meets the side. | [G·ship] |
| **Companion** | Falconer: a wooden porch over the entrance to the master's cabin. Requires a cabin. *Companionway* is a later compound. | [G·ship]; *companionway* [V] |
| **Scupper** | Falconer: leaden tubes carrying water off the decks through the ship's sides. An open boat has none; she is **bailed**. | [G·ship] |
| **Limbers**, **limber holes** | Falconer: square holes through the lower parts of the floor timbers near the keel, to let bilge water run to the pump. Related: **limber strake**, **limber board**, **limber rope**. | [G] |
| **Stanchion** | Falconer, in the AWNING entry: a range of light posts erected along the ship's side. Also the short pillars under beams, and the uprights of a rail. | [G] |
| **Bulwark(s)** | The solid side carried up above the deck. Implies a ship-scale structure of stanchions, planking and spirketting. | [G·ship] |
| **Washboard** | A light board set up above a boat's gunwale to keep out spray. **The right word for the low raised side of a small open boat.** | [G·boat] |
| **Weather cloth** | Painted canvas spread as a screen to shelter a crew. | [G] |
| **Gunwale**, **gunnel** | Steel: the horizontal plank covering the heads of the timbers. In a boat, the upper edge of the side, on which the oar bears. *Gunnel* is the spoken spelling and is good. | [G] |
| **Plank-sheer** | A ship's plank covering the timber heads. **Covering board** is the later yachting equivalent. | *plank-sheer* [G]; *covering board* [V/M] |
| **Rowlock**, **row-lock** | Falconer: in lighters and boats always termed the row-lock — **a notch or opening cut in the gunwale** for the oar to bear in. **Not a metal crutch.** | notch [G·boat]; metal fitting [V]; *oarlock* [A] |
| **Thole**, **thole pins** | Pins in a boat's gunwale retaining the oars when pulling. Ancient and plain. Either a pair, or one thole with a rope **grommet** round the oar. | [G·boat] |
| **Poppet** | Any short upright piece — beside a rowlock, or in a launching cradle. | [G] |
| **Grommet**, **strop** | A rope ring; round an oar at a thole, or round a block. | [G] |
| **Sweep** | Falconer: large oars used in small vessels to force them ahead. For a 53-ft vessel with 20½-ft oars, **sweeps** is correct. | [G] |
| **Oar** | The rower's implement in a boat. Its parts: the **handle**, the **loom** (shaft), and the **blade** or **wash**. | [G] |
| **To pull** | Period for "to row". Seamen pull an oar. | [G] |
| **Baler**, **piggin** | A small bailing vessel. Important in an open boat with no scuppers. | [G·boat] |
| **Ballast** | Weight in the bottom for stability. **Kentledge** is pig-iron ballast, ship-scale. | [G] |
| **Rudder**, **rudder head** | Hung on the sternpost by gudgeons and pintles. | [G] |
| **Tiller** | The lever shipped in the rudder head to steer by. | [G] |
| **Helm** | The whole steering apparatus, or the act of steering. | [G] |
| **Yoke** | A crosshead on a boat's rudder, steered by lines. Later boat fitting. | [V] |
| **Painter** | The rope at a boat's bow for making fast. | [G·boat] |
| **Grapnel** | A small multi-fluked anchor; the ground tackle of small craft. | [G] |

## 5. Guns and their furniture

| Term | Definition | Confidence |
|---|---|---|
| **Bracket**, **cheek** | A carriage's two sides. | [G] |
| **Transom** (of a carriage) | A carriage's cross pieces. **A false friend** — in 1776 *transoms* are also the timbers across the sternpost, and never the flat stern itself. | [G] |
| **Truck** | A carriage **wheel**. Also the disc at a masthead, and the rollers on a parrel. Falconer: a piece of wood conical, cylindrical, spherical or spheroidical. **Never the whole carriage.** | [G] |
| **Axletree** | A truck's axle. | [G] |
| **Trunnion** | The cylindrical lugs on which a gun pivots. | [G] |
| **Capsquare** | The iron over a trunnion holding it in its bed. | [G] |
| **Quoin** | The wedge under the breech, setting elevation. It sits on the **stool bed**. | [G] |
| **Breeching** | The heavy rope limiting recoil. | [G] |
| **Side tackle**, **train tackle** | The working tackles — running out, and hauling in. | [G] |
| **To train** | To turn a gun to its bearing. A gun is *trained*, not "aimed by bearing". | [G] |
| **Slide**, **bed** | The inclined baulk a sliding carriage runs on, as in the galley manner. A bearer laid on the floors for it is properly a **sleeper**; a timber laid to take wear under a travelling weight is a **skid** (period **skeed**). | [G] |
| **Platform** (gun) | The correct period word for a mortar, pivot or traversing mount. | [G] |
| **Slide carriage** | Best attested from the later 1770s on — carronades, galley and gunboat bow guns. Contemporary with 1776, not earlier: new technology at the time. | [G, new in period] |
| **Gunport** | An opening cut in the **side** for a broadside gun. | [G] |
| **Chase port** | The port a chase gun fires through, forward or aft. **What a bow chaser firing over the stem needs**, not a gunport. | [G] |
| **Bow chaser**, **stern chaser** | Guns firing forward and aft. | [G] |
| **Swivel** | A light gun shipping in a **socket** or **crutch** in the rail. Falconer's second sense of **crotch** is exactly this fitting: wood or iron opening into two horns like a half-moon. The bored timber is a **swivel stock**. | crotch [G]; *swivel stock* [?] |
| **Cascabel**, **base ring**, **swell**, **breech**, **muzzle**, **bore**, **vent**, **calibre** | The gun's own anatomy. | [G] |

### CAD word → period word

| Modeller's word | Period term |

## 6. Mast, spars, rigging and sails

| Term | Definition | Confidence |
|---|---|---|
| **Pole mast** | A mast made in one piece, as against a **made mast** built up of several. | [G] |
| **Step** | The mortise, in the keelson or a block on it, that the mast's heel stands in. | [G] |
| **Heel**, **head** | The lower and upper ends of a mast. | [G] |

| **Tack** | The rope hauling the weather clew of a course forward and down. | [G] |
| **Bowline**, **bridles** | Ropes from the bridles on a sail's leech, hauling it forward when close-hauled. | [G] |

| **Clew**, **leech**, **head**, **foot**, **bunt** | A sail's corners and edges. | [G] |

| **Drop** (of a sail) | Steel 1794, verbatim: "A term sometimes used to courses and topsails instead of depth." Period, but the loose variant even then. **Prefer *depth*.** | [G, secondary] |
| **Cringle** | A rope eye worked into the edge of a sail. | [G] |
| **Earing** | Falconer: small cords fastening a sail's upper corners to its yard, spliced to a cringle and passed several times round. | [G] |
| **Bolt rope** | The rope sewn round a sail's edges. | [G] |

| **Lining** | Steel: pieces of canvas, a sixth to a whole breadth, sewn across a sail to strengthen it. Named by position — **leech-lining**, **top-lining**, **buntline-cloth**, **mast-cloth**. **Broad bands, not a corner.** | [G] |
| **Clue-piece** | **The reinforcing piece at a lower corner** (the clew). Steel gives about two yards. | [G] |
| **Earing-piece** | **The small reinforcing piece at a head corner**, where the earing takes the sail to the yard. | [G] |
| **Peek-piece**, **nock-piece** | The same at the upper outer and upper inner corners of a **fore-and-aft** sail. | [G] |
| **Cloth** | One width of canvas in a sail — "a sail of twelve cloths". **Not the sail's body.** | [G] |
| **Reef band**, **reef points** | For shortening sail. | [G] |
| **Shroud** | Standing rigging from masthead to the side, supporting the mast laterally. | [G] |
| **Stay**, **forestay** | Standing rigging supporting the mast fore and aft. | [G] |
| **Deadeye** | Falconer: a round flattish wooden block pierced with three holes to receive a laniard. | [G] |
| **Heart** | A heart-shaped block with one large hole, used chiefly in setting up stays. | [G] |
| **Laniard** | The small rope rove through deadeyes or hearts to set rigging taut. Period spelling. | [G] |
| **Chains**, **chain plate**, **channel** (**chain-wale**) | The ironwork and projecting ledge by which a ship's shrouds set up to her side. On a small craft the shrouds set up to **ringbolts** in the sheer, or are lashed. | [G·ship] |
| **Ratlines** | Small lines across the shrouds, forming a ladder. Only if the mast is climbed. | [G] |
| **Woolding** | Rope turns bound tightly round a made mast or a sprung spar. | [G] |
| **Mast hoop** | An iron hoop round a mast. | [G] |
| **Sprit** | The spar spreading a spritsail diagonally from low on the mast to the sail's peak. Very much a small-craft rig. | [G·boat] |
| **Gaff**, **boom** | Spars of a fore-and-aft rig. | [G] |
| **Lateen** | A long yard slung obliquely, carrying a triangular sail. Mediterranean. | [G] |
| **Settee sail** | A lateen with the forward corner cut off, so quadrilateral. Mediterranean. | [G] |

**A square sail's corner is four things:** the **earing-piece** or **clue-piece**
(the patch), the **cringle** (the eye worked into it), the **bolt rope** (the
edging it is seized to), and the **earing** (the lashing to the yard). There is
no period generic covering both corner pieces — Steel names them by position — so
a model that prints one identical patch at all four corners has no period word
for it, and should say so rather than borrow one.

## 7. Awnings and shelter

The thinnest section for period evidence, so the confidence codes matter most.
Falconer's AWNING entry (1769) is the single most useful citation, and is worth
having in full:

> "a canopy of canvass extending over the decks of a ship in hot weather, for
> the convenience of the officers and crew, and to preserve the decks from being
> cracked or split by the heat of the sun. The awning is supported by a range of
> light posts, called **stanchions**, which are erected along the ship's side on
> the right and left; it is also suspended in the middle by a complication of
> small cords, called a **crowfoot**."

| Term | Definition | Confidence |
|---|---|---|
| **Awning** | As above. The safest and most general word. | [G] |
| **Stanchion** | Falconer's own word for an awning's uprights. | [G] |
| **Crowfoot** | Falconer: the complication of small cords suspending an awning in the middle. **The Georgian answer to a "ridge rope".** | [G] |
| **Ridge rope** | Smyth 1867: an awning's centre rope. **Not** in Falconer, which gives the crowfoot instead. | [V, probably earlier] |
| **Ridge** | The centre line of an awning, along its highest part. Ordinary English of any date — but implies a peak. | [G] as plain English |
| **Side ropes** | With the ridge rope, the ropes securing an awning's edges. | [V] |
| **Tilt** | Smyth: a light awning over the stern-sheets of a boat. Good English for a canopy — over a cart, a barge, a boat — since the 16th c.; its nautical-dictionary attestation is 1867. Defensible and evocative; *awning* is safer. | [V] in the dictionaries, [G] as English |
| **Bails**, **bailes** | Smyth: the hoops which bear up the tilt of a boat. The same word as a cart-tilt's bails, so probably older. **Only for hoops** — a straight crossbar is not a bail. | [V, probably earlier] |
| **Crotch**, **crutch** | Falconer: pieces of wood or iron whose upper part opens into two horns like a half-moon. **The period word for a forked support** — for a boom, a spare spar, a swivel. | [G] |
| **Stops** | Short pieces of rope-yarn or sennit tying a thing temporarily. Solid period usage, though Falconer's headword is STOPPER (the cable rope). | [G] as usage |
| **Lacing**, **to lace** | The line rove through eyelet holes along an awning's edge. | [G] |
| **Eyelet hole**, **grommet** | The worked holes in an awning's edge. | [G] |
| **Weather cloth** | Painted canvas spread as a screen against spray or sun. | [G] |
| **Tarpaulin**, **tarpawling** | Tarred canvas, spread over hatches and gratings. | [G] |
| **Fascine** | A bundle of brushwood bound together. Period military engineering; *Philadelphia* probably carried fascines along her sides against musket fire. | [G] |
| **the top surface** | **No period word exists.** Falconer names the stanchions and the crowfoot and nothing for the canvas's upper surface, because the awning *is* the canvas. Express it as a height. **Crown** is not it — see the false friends. | — |

| Modeller's word | Period term |
|---|---|
| canopy, sunshade, cover | **awning**; over a boat's stern sheets, a **tilt** |
| tent pole, vertical support | **stanchion** |
| forked support, Y-bracket | **crotch** (**crutch**) |
| ridge beam | **the ridge**; its hoops, **bails** |
| centre suspension lines | **crowfoot** (Georgian); *ridge rope* (Victorian) |
| guy line, edge line | **side ropes**, **lacings** |
| corner tie-down | **earing**, through a **cringle** or **eyelet hole** |
| zip tie, small lashing | **stops** of rope-yarn, **seizings** |

| Term | Definition | Confidence |

| **Eyebolt** | Steel: a bolt with an eye at the head, for tackles to hook to. | [G] |

| **Staple** | A U-shaped iron driven into timber. | [G] |
| **Gudgeon** (dockyard **googing**, **goodgeon**) | The iron braces bolted to the sternpost, each holed to receive a pintle. | [G] |
| **Pintle** | Steel: straps fastened on the rudder, each with a downward pin dropping into a gudgeon. | [G] |
| **Band** | An iron hoop shrunk or bolted round a spar, timber head or knee. | [G] |
| **Hoop** | An iron ring — round a mast, a bowsprit, a cask. | [G] |

| **Cleat** | A wedge-shaped or horned piece fastened to a spar, beam or side, for belaying; also small cleats stopping rigging from slipping. | [G] |
| **Kevel**, **cavil** | Steel: oak plank shaped like timber heads and fixed into mortises — the heavy belaying timber for tacks and sheets. A **kevel-head** is a timber head left long for the same. | [G] |
| **Timber head** | The top of a frame timber left standing above the gunwale, belayed to. **Far likelier than proper bitts on a hastily built boat** — and it is free structure. | [G] |
| **Bitts** | Steel: a frame of oak timber whereon the cables are fastened. A pair of uprights through the deck with a **cross piece**. **Riding bitts** take the cable. | [G] |

| **Hawse**, **hawse hole** | Steel: the situation of the cables before a vessel's stem. The **hawse holes** are bored in the bows for the cables. *Hawse pipe* is iron-ship usage. | [G]; *hawse pipe* [M] |

## The false friends

Words that sound period and are not, with what to use instead. Several of these
were reached for while naming the *Philadelphia* and rejected on checking.

| Avoid | Why | Use |
|---|---|---|
| **tumblehome** | one-word noun is modern | **tumbling home**, **tumbling in**, **falling home** |
| **flare** (noun) | Victorian as a noun; the verb is fine | **flaring** |
| **deadrise** | modern powerboat | **dead rising**, **the rising line of the floor** |
| **buttock lines** | 19th-c. draughting convention | **diagonals**, **horizontal lines** |
| **camber** (of a deck) | in the 18th c. usually the keel's fore-and-aft arc | **the round-up of the beam** |
| **table of offsets** | 19th-c. | **the draught**, **the scantling list** |
| **scrieve board** | not in Steel 1805 | **the mould loft floor**, **laying off** |
| **freeboard** | 19th-c. | "the height of the side above water" |
| **chine log** | 19th-c. American scow building | describe it, or **chine piece** with a caveat |
| **oarlock**, metal **rowlock** | Victorian fitting / American word | **row-lock** as a notch, or **thole pins** |
| **capping**, **cap rail**, **inwale**, **outwale** | boatbuilding and yachting | **gunwale**, **washboard**, **rising** |
| **covering board** | later / yachting | **plank-sheer** |
| **fairlead**, **fair-leader** | 19th-c., not in Steel 1805 | **bull's-eye**, **leading block**, **sheave hole**, **thimble** |
| **hawse pipe** | iron-ship era | **hawse hole** |
| **companionway** | later compound, and needs a cabin | **scuttle**, **hatchway** |
| **stringer** | 19th-c. iron shipbuilding | **clamp**, **shelf**, **sleeper**, **thick stuff** |
| **bottom boards** | standard boat English, not a Falconer headword | **foot-waling** |
| **crown** (of an awning) | the nautical crown belongs to an **anchor**; *crowning* is done to a rope's end. No period use for a canopy's top | a height, not a part |
| **bail** (for a straight member) | bails are **hoops** | **crossbar**, or name it plainly |
| **cloth** (for a sail's body) | a *cloth* is one width of canvas in a sail | **the sail**, **the canvas** |
| **drop** (of a sail) | period but the loose variant | **depth** |
| **lining**, **tabling** (for a corner) | linings are broad bands, tabling is the edge hem | **clue-piece**, **earing-piece** |
| **transom** (for the flat stern) | in 1776, transoms are timbers across the sternpost, and a gun carriage's cross pieces | **the stern**, **the fashion pieces** |
| **truck** (for a carriage) | a truck is a carriage **wheel**, or a masthead cap | **carriage** |
| **rig** (for anything but masts and sails) | a rig is a vessel's masts, spars and sails | name the thing |
| **floor** (for a surface) | a floor is a transverse bottom timber | **ceiling**, **foot-waling** |
| **frame** (for any framework) | a frame is a transverse hull timber | name the thing |
| **bearing** (for any angle) | a bearing is a compass direction from the observer | **training** (a gun), or just the angle |
| **rib** | layman's word | **timber**, **frame**, **futtock** |
| **porthole** | modern | **port** (gun), **light** or **scuttle** (window) |
| **deckhead**, **cabin sole** | yachting | "the underside of the deck"; **the platform** |
| **halyard**, **lanyard**, **rebate** | modern spellings | **halliard**, **laniard**, **rabbet** |
| **beam** (for the width) | period only in phrases — "on the beam", "beam ends" | **the breadth** (extreme or moulded) |
| **to row** | landsman's verb | **to pull** |
| **spreaders**, **jackstay** (of an awning) | not period at all | **bails**, **crowfoot** |

### Genuinely Georgian, but ship-scale

These are correct 18th-century words that a small open vessel simply did not
have. If one turns up in a model of a boat, it is probably describing something
simpler: **wales**, **scuppers**, **channels**, **chain plates**, **cant
timbers**, **deadwood**, **jeers**, **companion**, **false keel**,
**spirketting**, **clamp**, **ratlines**, **sheathing**, **kentledge**.

## Sources

Primary and near-primary, in rough order of value:

- **William Falconer, *An Universal Dictionary of the Marine* (1769; 1780 edn.)**
  — the single best source. Full text at Project Gutenberg and whalesite.org; the
  South Seas web edition gives the 1780 entries individually.
- **David Steel, *The Elements and Practice of Rigging and Seamanship* (1794),
  part 4** — a *sailmaker's and rigger's* text, and far better than his 1805 book
  on sails. The source for *depth*, *drop*, the corner pieces, the linings and
  tabling. Transcription at legacy.maritime.org.
- **David Steel, *Elements and Practice of Naval Architecture* (1805)**, "An
  Explanation of the Terms Used in Ship Building" — best on timbers, framing,
  lofting and ironwork. Transcription at hmssurprise.org; note it is abridged
  for A–C, so an absence there is not evidence.
- **Mungo Murray, *A Treatise on Shipbuilding and Navigation* (1754).**
- **R. H. Dana, *The Seaman's Friend* (1841)** — useful for American usage and
  for dating.
- **W. H. Smyth, *The Sailor's Word-Book* (1867)** — used mainly to *date* terms.
  What appears here and **not** in Falconer is Victorian, not Georgian. That is
  how *tilt*, *bails*, *rising* and *ridge rope* were placed.

On *Philadelphia* and her type: Smithsonian NMAH's Gunboat *Philadelphia*
Preservation Project; the Lake Champlain Maritime Museum; and the two documents
that would give her *archaeologically recorded* member names and scantlings —
Philip Lundeberg, *The Gunboat Philadelphia and the Defense of Lake Champlain in
1776*, and the National Historic Landmark nomination (NRHP 66000852). **Neither
has been mined.** Start there if a future model needs real counts and scantlings
rather than general period practice.
